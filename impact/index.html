How this work has been used

A collection like this one is worth what other people can do with it. This page is the record: who has taken the datasets and used them for a different question, who has cited the papers, and where the work has been picked up outside universities. Every claim below names the document it comes from, so it can be checked rather than believed.

Reused as data

Slichter and Tran, who have no connection to this project, ask whether better journals publish better estimates, and answer it with 14,387 published estimates from 24 literatures. They name three sources for those literatures, and this site is one of them: “a database of meta-analyses operated by the Institute of Economic Studies at Charles University”, with meta-analysis.cz in the footnote. Eleven of their twenty-four literatures are datasets from this site, and those eleven supply 502 of the 871 primary studies in their sample. Their finding is that within a literature the mean and variance of published estimates have little or no correlation with journal rank: the chance that an estimate from a top journal is closer to the truth than one from a mid-ranked journal is about 51%, a coin flip. The paper is accepted at the Journal of Political Economy: Microeconomics; until it appears there it is IZA Discussion Paper 17960. That is the use the pooled table exists for, and so far it is the one paper that has used the collection at that scale.

Cited

The 71 papers collected here are cited in the empirical literature, in handbooks, and in graduate reading lists; Havranek’s Google Scholar profile, which is made up of them, records more than 10,000 citations. The most common use is the simplest one: someone needs a single number, and takes it from a synthesis of a literature rather than from any one study. The number is usually the same one, the mean reported elasticity of intertemporal substitution, 0.5, from the cross-country meta-analysis of 2,735 estimates from 169 studies covering 104 countries. Thomas Sargent and John Stachurski cite it for a plausible average in Dynamic Programming (Cambridge University Press): “the detailed meta-analysis by Havranek et al. (2015) suggests 0.5 as a plausible average value for international studies, with rich countries tending slightly higher.” Simon Johnson, Lukasz Rachel, and Catherine Wolfram set its inverse to 2 in A Theory of Price Caps on Nonrenewable Resources, American Economic Review 116(7): 2711-2753, calling it “the mean value in the influential meta-study of Havranek, Horvath, Irsova, and Rusnak (2015)”. Both are citing the mean of what has been published, which the companion paper on publication bias argues is inflated by selective reporting: corrected, it is closer to 0.3 to 0.4.

Tomohiro Hirano and Joseph Stiglitz cite that paper, the companion paper, and the capital-labor substitution meta-analysis in Overlapping generations models, multiplicity of steady states and momentary equilibria, and economic fluctuations, Oxford Review of Economic Policy, where the capital-labor elasticity decides whether their model has one steady state or several. Michael Kremer, Gautam Rao, and Frank Schilbach point to the publication-bias paper as the review of the literature behind standard macro calibrations of the elasticity, in Behavioral development economics, Handbook of Behavioral Economics. David Card, Jochen Kluve, and Andrea Weber cite it for the opposite reason, as a contrast to the funnel plot they find in their own meta-analysis of active labor market programs in the Journal of the European Economic Association; Card’s graduate labor economics course at Berkeley read it in its 2016 lecture on intertemporal labor supply and reproduced its funnel plot, Lecture 5 of Economics 250A. Sargent, Johnson, Stiglitz, Kremer, and Card hold the Nobel Prize in economics.

In policy institutions

Some of this work is used for its numbers and some for its method, and the second kind is the more telling: institutions that run their own reviews copy the procedures here, how to search for studies, how to date them, how to test for publication bias, how to average across models. The International Monetary Fund, reviewing the effects of macroprudential policy across more than 6,000 estimates, builds its search that way and cites four of these papers: “the paper selection approach is similar to Havranek and Sokolova (2020) and Havranek and Irsova (2011)” (IMF Working Paper 20/67). A World Bank policy research working paper on agglomeration economies in developing countries runs its publication-bias tests “following Havranek, 2015” and averages models the way the habit-formation meta-analysis does (Policy Research Working Paper 9730). A European Commission Joint Research Centre report, on what closing schools during Covid did to student performance, dates its studies the way the borders meta-analysis does (JRC134506). The OECD’s 2025 review of what climate policies do to emissions lists the gasoline meta-analysis among the syntheses of how fuel use responds to fuel prices (IFCMA Papers).

Others cite the findings. One meta-analysis turns up more than the rest, the pair on vertical and horizontal productivity spillovers from foreign investment, because it answers a question development institutions have to take a position on. Each entry below is one document, published by the institution named, that cites a paper from this site.

  • UNCTAD, World Investment Report 2013, the UN’s annual report on foreign investment, citing the vertical spillovers meta-analysis.
  • World Trade Organization, Global Value Chain Development Report 2025, on FDI and value chain integration, and the 2021 edition before it.
  • Asian Development Bank, an ADB Institute working paper on remittances and growth in Nepal, citing the remittances meta-analysis.
  • Inter-American Development Bank, Boosting Foreign Direct Investment through Strategic Reforms: “Havranek and Irsova (2011) covering 47 countries and 57 studies find robust evidence that upstream vertical effects are positive and significant.”
  • UNU-WIDER, a working paper on how foreign investment changes the complexity of South Africa’s exports.
  • CPB Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis, a background document for its advice on pensions and low returns, which sets the elasticity of intertemporal substitution to 0.5, “the mean of the values estimated in the literature”.
  • The Treasury of New Zealand, International Investment for Growth, citing the horizontal spillovers meta-analysis.
  • Statistics Norway, a discussion paper on the substitution and income effects of labor income taxation.
  • Institute for Fiscal Studies, a working paper on the gender wage gap, which uses the skill substitution meta-analysis to describe what the literature estimates and how.
  • DIW Berlin, its report on carbon pricing in heating and transport, which tabulates the gasoline price elasticity from this site among the estimates it works from.
  • Bruegel, a working paper on knowledge spillovers and geopolitical risk in supply chains, citing both FDI meta-analyses.
  • ZEW, a discussion paper on profit shifting by multinationals, which follows the sampling convention of the vertical spillovers meta-analysis.

Legislatures use the results themselves. The Congressional Research Service has cited the daylight saving meta-analysis in the report it prepares for the US Congress in every version since 2018, most recently the July 2026 update. The European Parliamentary Research Service used the same paper in the ex-post impact assessment of the EU summer-time directive written for the European Parliament’s Legal Affairs Committee, calling it “a seemingly methodologically sound literature review of the available research”.

Central banks

The densest cluster is in central banks, which is what you would expect of a collection with this many meta-analyses of monetary policy in it. Each entry is one document published by the bank named.

  • European Central Bank, Monetary policy transmission: a reference guide through ESCB models and empirical benchmarks, Occasional Paper 377, which cites the meta-analysis of transmission lags, the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, and the forward premium puzzle: “according to a meta-analysis conducted by Havranek and Rusnak (2013), researchers who use monthly instead of quarterly data systematically report a faster MP transmission.”
  • Bank for International Settlements, the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision’s literature review on financial technology and competition for banking services, on bank competition and financial stability: “as pointed out in a meta-analysis by Zigraiova and Havranek (2016), there is no general consensus”. A BIS working paper cites the finance and growth meta-analysis.
  • Federal Reserve Board, Modeling the Consumption Response to the CARES Act, on habit formation in consumption.
  • Bank of England, a staff working paper on bank competition and risk in the United Kingdom: “Zigraiova and Havranek (2016) use meta-data analysis to examine almost 600 empirical estimates from 31 studies of the competition-stability link and find no definitive systematic evidence.”
  • Swiss National Bank, a working paper on the interest rate sensitivity of house prices, which benchmarks its result against the house price meta-analysis: “the existing literature, which, according to a meta-study by Ehrenbergerova et al. (2022), suggests an average peak decline of 1.2% after just two years.”
  • Reserve Bank of Australia, Anticipatory Monetary Policy and the ‘Price Puzzle’, on the price puzzle meta-analysis: “in a survey of 70 studies using VARs spanning 31 countries, Rusnak, Havranek and Horvath (2013) found that half showed a price puzzle.”
  • Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Subjective Intertemporal Substitution: “a recent meta study by Havranek (2015) based on 2,735 estimates from 169 published papers reports a distribution of estimates that ranges from -5 to 5.”
  • Deutsche Bundesbank, a discussion paper on markups and financial shocks, and Banque de France, a working paper on credit risk and bank competition in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Banca d’Italia, a paper on capital misallocation and financial development, and Banco de España, a working paper on time variation in vector autoregressions.
  • Sveriges Riksbank, its Economic Review on the global fall in real interest rates, and Bank of Canada, a staff working paper on vector autoregressions.
  • Bank of Japan, its early warning system for financial imbalances, which uses the crisis early warning dataset.
  • And De Nederlandsche Bank, Norges Bank, the Central Bank of Ireland, the Oesterreichische Nationalbank, the National Bank of Belgium, the Bank of Greece, Eesti Pank, the Bank of Israel, the European Stability Mechanism, the EBRD, and the Federal Reserve Banks of St. Louis, San Francisco, Dallas, Boston, and Cleveland.

In the Czech case the evidence went into the room where the decision was made. From 2015 to 2019 Havranek was full-time Advisor to the Board of the Czech National Bank, and the opinions he wrote for the Board argue from meta-analytic averages. The opinion on Situation Report 6/2017 makes the case for a steeper path of rate increases partly on the ground that “the average of published estimates of transmission in the Czech Republic suggests that a change in rates has its greatest effect on prices after 15 months”, citing the meta-analysis of transmission lags. The Bank has since released those opinions, and they are here in English translation beside the Czech originals it published: 2/2016, 6/2016, 6/2017, and 8/2018.

Taught

Meta-Analysis of Social Science Research: A Practitioner’s Guide is the teaching form of this work, and the reporting standards that go with it are the MAER-Net guidelines. The methods are also taught as courses by invitation; the most recent was two days for policy economists at the Swedish Agency for Growth Policy Analysis in Stockholm in August 2025, whose programme says which sessions were hands-on in Stata and in R. MAIVE, the estimator that reduces the bias arising when reported precision is spurious, runs in a browser at EasyMeta.org.
