This page goes from a download link to a defensible, bias-corrected estimate in about ten lines of code, using one literature from this site’s data: the price elasticity of gasoline demand. Every number printed below is computed from the shipped files of data release 1.1.1; paste the code and you will get the same numbers. The steps — look at the data, split what should not be pooled, test for publication bias, read the corrected estimate against the published paper — are the same for every literature in the table of 45 datasets.

Step 1. Load one literature

Each dataset ships as a small CSV with a machine-readable codebook. The gasoline literature is 202 price-elasticity estimates from 41 primary studies, collected for Havranek, Irsova & Janda (2012, Energy Economics):

import pandas as pd
d = pd.read_csv("https://meta-analysis.cz/data/v1/gasoline_price/gasoline_price.csv",
                float_precision="round_trip")
d.shape           # (202, 11)
d.idstudy.nunique()   # 41 studies
d.e.mean()        # -0.44

In R: d <- read.csv("https://meta-analysis.cz/data/v1/gasoline_price/gasoline_price.csv"). The float_precision argument matters only in pandas, whose default CSV parser is not exactly lossless; the Parquet files sidestep the issue entirely.

Step 2. Split what should not be pooled

The mean of −0.44 is the answer to no question, because the file mixes two different elasticities: the codebook’s longr flag separates the short-run response to a price change from the long-run response after the vehicle stock adjusts. Pooling them averages two populations. Split first:

short = d[d.longr == 0]   # 110 estimates, 30 studies, mean -0.23, median -0.19
long_ = d[d.longr == 1]   #  92 estimates, 30 studies, mean -0.69, median -0.63

The same trap scales up: in the site-wide pooled table an elasticity, a partial correlation and a dollar value sit in the same effect column, and effect_units records which is which. Serious analysis happens within a literature, on the per-dataset file with its full set of moderators. The pooled table is for cross-literature questions — distributions of t-statistics, prevalence of publication bias — where the unit mixing is the point, not a bug.

Step 3. Test for publication bias

The workhorse test is a regression you can explain in one sentence: if a literature reports estimates regardless of their sign and significance, reported effects should not correlate with their standard errors; if researchers or journals prefer significant negative elasticities, imprecise studies will report systematically more negative numbers. The funnel-asymmetry and precision-effect test (FAT-PET; Stanley 2005) runs the regression in its precision-weighted form, t = FAT + PET·(1/SE): the intercept measures selective reporting, and the coefficient on precision is the estimate of the effect corrected for it. Standard errors must be clustered by study — estimates from one study share data and methods and are not independent observations:

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
m = smf.ols("t ~ prec", data=short).fit(cov_type="cluster",
                                        cov_kwds={"groups": short.idstudy})
print(m.summary())
# the same in R; fixest's default small-sample adjustment matches the table exactly
summary(fixest::feols(t ~ prec, data = short, cluster = ~idstudy))

The results survive the standard robustness checks. Winsorising both variables at the 1st and 99th centiles moves nothing that matters (short-run PET stays at −0.065; long-run PET goes to −0.21). The small-sample cluster correction CR2 (Bell–McCaffrey, refined by Pustejovsky & Tipton 2018 and implemented in R’s clubSandwich) widens the standard errors moderately — to 0.017 for the short-run PET and 0.155 for the long-run PET — which leaves the short-run conclusions intact and makes the long-run corrected effect statistically indistinguishable from zero. With 30 clusters, that correction is worth reporting, not optional.

Step 4. Read the answer

Both intercepts are large and negative: the literature selectively reports negative, significant price elasticities, and the bias is severe by the standard yardstick, which reads |FAT| above 2 as severe selectivity (Doucouliagos & Stanley 2013). Correcting for it cuts the short-run elasticity from a reported mean of −0.23 to −0.065, and the long-run from −0.69 to roughly −0.2 with a wide confidence band. The published paper reaches the same verdict with heavier machinery — mixed-effects multilevel meta-regression yields corrected elasticities of −0.09 and −0.31 and the abstract’s headline that published averages are “exaggerated twofold”. The simple two-step regression on this page reproduces the direction, the significance, and the order of magnitude of that correction; the remaining gap between PET and the paper’s preferred numbers is the estimator, and it is why FAT-PET is where a meta-analysis starts, not where it ends. Where to go from here: the Practitioner’s Guide for the complete 18-point checklist (heterogeneity, model averaging, p-hacking tests), and MAIVE for what to do when reported standard errors themselves cannot be trusted.

If you pool across literatures instead, two rules from the data documentation prevent silent errors: cluster on the pair (dataset, study_id), never on study_id alone — study identifiers restart in every literature, and the bare column would merge unrelated studies and collapse 2,963 clusters into 524; and deduplicate on (dataset, estimate_id), which is unique by construction.
