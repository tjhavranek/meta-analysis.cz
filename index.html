Data and codes for meta-analysis

This platform is a digital repository for meta-analysis methods and applications co-authored by researchers at the Faculty of Social Sciences, Charles University, Prague. Meta-analysis, the quantitative approach to research synthesis, was originally developed in the behavioral and medical sciences. In economics and other social sciences, it has become a valuable tool for synthesizing evidence, correcting publication bias, and linking differences in primary study results to their contextual backgrounds. The papers collected here have appeared in journals such as Nature Communications, Review of Economics and Statistics, Journal of the European Economic Association, Journal of Labor Economics, and Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics.

Benchmark findings from the meta-analyses on this site

One line per meta-analysis: the value the literature settles on once publication bias is taken into account, where the evidence shows such bias. Each line names the authors of that study and links to its data, code and paper. These are summaries of a literature, not universal constants — definitions, samples and applicable contexts differ, and some entries are historical rather than current benchmarks, so follow the link before using a number.

  • Armington elasticity (substitution between domestic and foreign goods): the meta-analytic estimate is 2.5-5.1, median 3.8, based on 3,524 estimates (Bajzik et al. 2020, Journal of International Economics) doi.
  • Beauty premium in earnings: the meta-analytic estimate is 1.1% (95% CrI -0.8% to 3.0%) (controlling for cognitive ability; excludes sex workers), based on 1,159 estimates in 67 studies (Irsova et al. 2025).
  • Border effect on international trade: the meta-analytic finding is one-third reduction in trade, based on 1,271 estimates from 61 studies (Havranek and Irsova 2017, IMF Economic Review) doi.
  • Climate sensitivity (warming from a doubling of CO2): the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 1.4-2.3 degrees Celsius (historical estimate; not the current IPCC assessment (AR6: ~3 C)), based on 48 estimates from 16 studies (Reckova and Irsova 2015, Energy and Environment) doi.
  • Coefficient of relative risk aversion: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 1 in economics, 2-7 in finance (from consumption Euler equation studies), based on 1,021 estimates from 92 studies (Elminejad et al. 2025, Journal of Economic Surveys) doi.
  • Effect of bank competition on financial stability: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is about 0, based on 598 estimates from 31 studies (Zigraiova and Havranek 2016, Journal of Economic Surveys) doi.
  • Effect of board gender diversity on ESG performance: the meta-analytic estimate is about 0.12 ESG points per 1-pp diversity increase (ESG scored 0-100; higher in the Middle East, about zero in Southeast Asia), based on 533 estimates from 106 studies (Hozova et al. 2026).
  • Effect of class size on student achievement: the meta-analytic estimate is negligible (about 0) (except STAR project and classes under 15 students), based on 2,819 estimates from 66 studies (Opatrny et al. 2025, Journal of Labor Economics) doi.
  • Effect of financial development on economic growth: the meta-analytic finding is positive and statistically significant (weaker in poor countries; declines after 1980s), based on 1334 estimates from 67 studies (Valickova et al. 2015, Journal of Economic Surveys) doi.
  • Effect of financial incentives on performance: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is about 0 (field contexts; lab and loss framing modestly positive), based on 2,193 estimates from 88 economics experiments (Cala et al. 2026, Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics, forthcoming).
  • Effect of monetary policy on house prices: the meta-analytic estimate is a 1.2% fall in house prices per 1-percentage-point policy rate rise, bottoming out after two years (mean reported response; the paper finds it exaggerated by publication bias), based on 1,555 estimates from 237 impulse responses in 37 studies (Ehrenbergerova et al. 2023, IMF Economic Review) doi.
  • Effect of physical exercise on cognition: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is SMD 0.227 (cognition), 0.027 (memory), 0.012 (executive function) (wide prediction intervals spanning negative and positive effects), based on 2,239 effect-size estimates from 215 meta-analyses (Bartoš et al. 2025).
  • Effect of remittances on economic growth: the meta-analytic finding is positive but economically small (growth-enhancing in Asia but not Africa), based on 538 estimates from 95 studies (Cazachevici et al. 2020, World Development) doi.
  • Effect of structural reforms on economic growth: the meta-analytic finding is costly short run, strongly positive long run (transition countries), based on 46 studies (Babecky and Havranek 2014, Economics of Transition) doi.
  • Effect of the euro on trade: the meta-analytic finding is statistically insignificant (other currency unions: more than 60%) (Havranek 2010, Review of World Economics) doi.
  • Effect of working while in school on educational outcomes: the meta-analytic estimate is about 0 (slightly negative) (more substantive for dropout decisions), based on 861 estimates from 69 studies (Kroupova et al. 2024, Economics of Education Review) doi.
  • Elasticity of intertemporal substitution in consumption: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 0.3-0.4 (micro estimates for asset holders; macro mean zero), based on 2,735 estimates from 169 published studies (Havranek 2015, Journal of the European Economic Association) doi.
  • Elasticity of substitution between capital and labor: the meta-analytic estimate is 0.3 (best-practice specification; reported mean is 0.9), based on 3,186 estimates from 121 studies (Gechert et al. 2022, Review of Economic Dynamics) doi.
  • Elasticity of substitution between native and immigrant workers: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is about 17 (baseline regional specification; simple bias correction implies 22), based on 1,091 estimates from 41 studies (Kantova et al. 2026).
  • Elasticity of substitution between skilled and unskilled labor: the meta-analytic estimate is 4 (lower bound 2) (smaller for developing countries) (Havranek et al. 2024, Review of Economics and Statistics) doi.
  • Electricity savings from daylight saving time: the meta-analytic estimate is 0.34% savings (mean reported estimate, during days when DST applies), based on 162 estimates from 44 studies (Havranek et al. 2018, Energy Journal) doi.
  • Excess sensitivity of consumption to anticipated income changes: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is about 0 (micro data, households with substantial liquidity), based on about 3,000 estimates from 144 studies (per the paper's title) (Havranek and Sokolova 2020, Review of Economic Dynamics) doi.
  • FDI spillovers to domestic firm productivity (Czech Republic): the meta-analytic estimate is +11% productivity per 10-percentage-point rise in foreign ownership (Czech Republic, as of 2018; 19% for joint ventures), based on 332 estimates (Hampl et al. 2020, Applied Economics) doi.
  • Forward premium puzzle (slope from regressions of spot on forward rates): the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 0.31 developed, 0.98 emerging currencies (unbiasedness hypothesis predicts slope of 1), based on 3,643 estimates from 91 studies (Zigraiova et al. 2021, European Economic Review) doi.
  • Frisch elasticity of labor supply (intertemporal substitution in hours worked): the meta-analytic estimate is about 0.25 (total hours) (intensive margin 0.2; extensive margin tiny), based on over 700 estimates for each margin (Elminejad et al. 2023, Review of Economic Dynamics) doi.
  • Habit formation in consumption: the meta-analytic estimate is 0.4 (macro 0.6, micro 0.1) (mean reported estimate, no bias correction in abstract), based on 597 estimates from 81 published studies (Havranek et al. 2017, European Economic Review) doi.
  • Hedge fund alpha (monthly risk-adjusted return): the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 30-40 basis points per month (before fees; the companion study finds about zero after fees and design), based on 1,019 alpha estimates from 74 studies (Yang et al. 2024, Journal of Economic Surveys) doi.
  • Hedge fund performance (alpha): the meta-analytic estimate is about 0 (after fees and best-practice design; gross alphas are 30-40 bp/month), based on 1,019 estimates from 74 studies (Yang et al. 2026).
  • Horizontal FDI spillovers to domestic firms' productivity: the meta-analytic estimate is 0 on average (varies with technology gap and ownership structure), based on 1205 estimates (Irsova and Havranek 2013, World Development) doi.
  • Income elasticity of gasoline demand: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 0.1 short run, 0.23 long run (Havranek and Kokes 2015, Energy Economics) doi.
  • Income elasticity of water demand: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is about 0.15 or less (Havranek et al. 2018, Land Economics) doi.
  • Individual discount rate (impatience measured in experiments): the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 33% per year (Matousek et al. 2022, Experimental Economics) doi.
  • Learning loss from COVID-19 school closures: the meta-analytic estimate is about -0.12 SD (roughly 30% of a school year) (Luskova et al. 2026).
  • Natural resource curse (effect of resource richness on long-run growth): the meta-analytic finding is weak support for a curse, based on 605 estimates from 43 studies (Havranek et al. 2016, World Development) doi.
  • Optimal long-run inflation rate: the meta-analytic estimate is about 0.6% per year (calibration-dominated model literature), based on 777 estimates from 116 primary studies (Opatrny et al. 2026).
  • Price elasticity of electricity demand: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is -0.16 short run, -0.38 long run (best-identified short-run estimates statistically indistinguishable from zero), based on 4,720 estimates from 462 studies (Kudela et al. 2026).
  • Price elasticity of gasoline demand: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is -0.09 short run, -0.31 long run (Havranek et al. 2012, Energy Economics) doi.
  • Sensitivity of meta-analysis results to outlier handling: the meta-analytic estimate is median shift at most 0.047 (Cohen's d); significance flips in 11.5% of cases (across four pre-registered outlier treatments and two estimators), based on 358 behavioral science meta-analyses (Havranek et al. 2026).
  • Size premium in stock returns: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 1.72% per year (smallest vs. largest capitalization quintile), based on 1,746 estimates from 102 published studies (Astakhov et al. 2019, Journal of Economic Surveys) doi.
  • Social cost of carbon: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 0-134 USD per metric ton of carbon (2010 prices, emission year 2015; per ton of carbon, not CO2), based on 809 estimates from 101 studies (Havranek et al. 2015, Energy Economics) doi.
  • Stock-price reaction to shareholder activism: the publication-bias-corrected estimate is 0% to 1.5%, based on 1,973 estimates from 67 studies (Bajzik et al. 2025, Corporate Governance: An International Review) doi.
  • The price puzzle (prices rising after monetary tightening): the meta-analytic finding is an artifact of model misspecification, based on about 1,000 estimates from 70 studies (Rusnak et al. 2013, Journal of Money, Credit and Banking) doi.
  • Transmission lag of monetary policy to prices: the meta-analytic estimate is 29 months on average (maximum price decline 1.2% after 1-pp hike), based on 67 published studies (Havranek and Rusnak 2013, International Journal of Central Banking).
  • Trust, rule of law, and the size premium in stock returns: the meta-analytic finding is stronger rule of law, larger size premium (association in reported estimates, not causal), based on 1,613 estimates from 105 studies and 31 countries (Schwarz et al. 2026).
  • Tuition-enrollment elasticity (effect of tuition on university enrollment): the publication-bias-corrected estimate is about 0, based on 443 estimates from 43 studies (Havranek et al. 2018, Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics) doi.
  • Vertical FDI spillovers to suppliers and buyers: the meta-analytic finding is significant to suppliers, small to buyers, based on 3,626 estimates (Havranek and Irsova 2011, Journal of International Economics) doi.

Methods behind these numbers: MAIVE corrects for spurious precision by instrumenting variance with sample size; partial correlations and conventional correlation meta-analysis are biased in ways we quantify; reporting and method guidelines set out the practice we follow.
